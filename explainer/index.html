How Index Rules Change What Your Passive Funds Own

Quick guide to fast-entry index rules.

Last updated: June 2026

1. "Passive" doesn't mean static

A passive fund — like an index ETF or index mutual fund — doesn't pick its own stocks. Instead, it follows the rules in a document called an index methodology, published by an index provider such as Nasdaq, S&P Dow Jones Indices, CRSP, FTSE Russell, or MSCI.

2. The Nasdaq-100: what it is and who it covers

The Nasdaq-100 Index tracks the 100 largest non-financial companies listed on the Nasdaq exchange, ranked by market capitalization. It's weighted by "modified market-cap" — a method that adjusts weights to prevent excessive concentration while still reflecting size.

  • QQQ (Invesco QQQ Trust) — the largest and most traded
  • QQQM (Invesco Nasdaq-100 ETF) — structurally identical, lower share price, lower expense ratio (0.15% vs. 0.20%)

3. The fast-entry rule: what changed on May 1, 2026

Before May 2026, a company had to be trading on an eligible exchange for a substantial period before becoming eligible for Nasdaq-100 inclusion. The annual reconstitution (December) and quarterly reviews were the primary on-ramps.

Note on sourcing: The mechanism below is based on reporting current as of June 2026. The official Nasdaq methodology document is the authoritative source. Verify any specific numbers against the published methodology before drawing conclusions. Nasdaq Index Services methodology — see Tracker for source links

4. The SpaceX case: a case study in progress

SpaceX (Space Exploration Technologies Corp.) has been reported to be targeting a Nasdaq listing in June 2026, potentially under the ticker SPCX, with a reported target listing date of approximately June 12, 2026.

5. The S&P 500 contrast: a different rulebook, a different timeline

The S&P 500 — tracked by VOO, IVV, and SPY — operates under fundamentally different inclusion criteria managed by S&P Dow Jones Indices. As of June 2026, S&P DJI has not adopted a fast-entry rule.

6. Total-market funds: a third timeline

Total-market funds like VTI (CRSP US Total Market) and ITOT (S&P Total Market) track broader indexes that include large, mid, small, and micro-cap stocks across all sectors. Their IPO inclusion policies differ from both the Nasdaq-100 and the S&P 500:

  • VTI / CRSP: New listings are generally added at the next quarterly reconstitution after the IPO. CRSP does not have a fast-entry rule equivalent to Nasdaq's.
  • ITOT / S&P Total Market: Governed by S&P DJI rules; follows a similar quarterly schedule. May add very large IPOs on a different schedule from the Nasdaq-100.

7. Target-date funds: the pass-through question

If your 401(k) is invested in a target-date fund (e.g., "2045 Fund" or "Retirement 2050"), you hold a fund-of-funds. The underlying funds — typically a mix of U.S. equity, international equity, and bond index funds — each have their own index methodology.
