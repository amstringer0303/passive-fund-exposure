How Index Rules Change What Your Passive Funds Own

A plain-language guide to the Nasdaq-100 fast-entry rule and what it means for retirement savers who hold index funds.

Last updated: June 2026 · Educational information only

1. "Passive" doesn't mean static

A passive fund — like an index ETF or index mutual fund — doesn't pick its own stocks. Instead, it follows the rules in a document called an index methodology, published by an index provider such as Nasdaq, S&P Dow Jones Indices, CRSP, FTSE Russell, or MSCI.

When you hold a passive fund, you're really holding whatever the current version of that rulebook says to hold. If the rulebook changes, the fund's holdings can change — without any decision by you, your fund manager, or your plan administrator.

The key insight: Your exposure to any particular company is determined first by whether that company is in your fund's index, and second by when it gets added. Both of those depend entirely on the index methodology rules — which can and do change.

This is not a design flaw. It's how passive investing is supposed to work. But it means that understanding the rulebook is part of understanding what you own.

2. The Nasdaq-100: what it is and who it covers

The Nasdaq-100 Index tracks the 100 largest non-financial companies listed on the Nasdaq exchange, ranked by market capitalization. It's weighted by "modified market-cap" — a method that adjusts weights to prevent excessive concentration while still reflecting size.

Two major ETFs track the Nasdaq-100 directly:

  • QQQ (Invesco QQQ Trust) — the largest and most traded
  • QQQM (Invesco Nasdaq-100 ETF) — structurally identical, lower share price, lower expense ratio (0.15% vs. 0.20%)

Many 401(k) menus include one or both of these. Large-cap growth ETFs (VUG, SCHG, IWF) and tech-sector ETFs (VGT, XLK, FTEC) may also acquire substantial positions in Nasdaq-100 members, though they're governed by different index rules.

3. The fast-entry rule: what changed on May 1, 2026

Before May 2026, a company had to be trading on an eligible exchange for a substantial period before becoming eligible for Nasdaq-100 inclusion. The annual reconstitution (December) and quarterly reviews were the primary on-ramps.

On May 1, 2026, Nasdaq Index Services adopted a fast-entry rule for large-cap IPOs with low public float. Here is how it works:

Note on sourcing: The mechanism below is based on reporting current as of June 2026. The official Nasdaq methodology document is the authoritative source. Verify any specific numbers against the published methodology before drawing conclusions. Nasdaq Index Services methodology — see Tracker for source links

Fast-entry eligibility criteria

  • Market-cap rank: The new listing must rank among the top 40 eligible non-members of the Nasdaq-100 by market capitalization at the time of assessment.
  • Assessment day: Nasdaq conducts the assessment on approximately the 7th trading day after the company's exchange listing.
  • Liquidity test: The stock must demonstrate at least $5,000,000 in average daily trading volume (ADTV) to qualify.
  • Low-float criterion: The rule is specifically designed for companies where public float is a small fraction of total shares outstanding — meaning most shares are held by insiders, employees, or pre-IPO investors and are not immediately tradeable.

Fast-entry addition mechanics

  • Addition day: If the company qualifies, it is added to the index on approximately the 15th trading day after listing.
  • Initial weight cap: Low-float names do not enter at their full market-cap weight. The initial weight is capped at approximately one-third of the float-adjusted market cap, or roughly three times the float cap — whichever is lower. This limits the immediate impact on QQQ and QQQM holders.
  • Weight ramp: The full market-cap weight is not applied immediately. Additional weight may be allocated at subsequent quarterly reconstitutions as the publicly tradeable float grows (e.g., as lockup periods expire for insiders and employees).
  • Deletion rules unchanged: Fast-entry additions are subject to the same standard deletion criteria at future reconstitutions as any other index member.

The practical effect: a qualifying IPO can appear in QQQ and QQQM within roughly three calendar weeks of listing.

4. The SpaceX case: a case study in progress

Status as of June 2026: The details below are based on press reporting and have not been confirmed via SEC EDGAR filings as of the publication date. An 8-A12B exchange registration filing and/or final prospectus (424B4) would be the authoritative sources. Check the Tracker for updates.

SpaceX (Space Exploration Technologies Corp.) has been reported to be targeting a Nasdaq listing in June 2026, potentially under the ticker SPCX, with a reported target listing date of approximately June 12, 2026.

If those reports are accurate and the listing proceeds as described, the fast-entry eligibility assessment would occur on approximately the 7th trading day following the listing (around June 23, 2026 if June 12 is the listing date), and addition to the Nasdaq-100 could follow around the 15th trading day (approximately July 3, 2026) — subject to passing the ADTV and market-cap rank tests.

Why SpaceX is a distinctive case

  • Dual-class share structure: SpaceX has been reported to have a share structure that concentrates voting control. The publicly traded shares would likely represent a small fraction of total equity, making it a prime candidate for the low-float fast-entry pathway.
  • Scale: Private-market valuations reported for SpaceX have been very large. If accurate, a public listing at such scale would rank it immediately among the largest Nasdaq-listed companies by market cap.
  • Lockup considerations: Employee and insider shares are typically subject to lockup agreements preventing sale for 90–180 days post-IPO. Float will expand as lockups expire, which would trigger further weight ramp-ups at subsequent reconstitutions.

This is a case study in how the fast-entry rule was designed to work: a very large company with a small initial float enters at a capped weight and grows its index presence as float expands.

5. The S&P 500 contrast: a different rulebook, a different timeline

The S&P 500 — tracked by VOO, IVV, and SPY — operates under fundamentally different inclusion criteria managed by S&P Dow Jones Indices. As of June 2026, S&P DJI has not adopted a fast-entry rule.

To be eligible for the S&P 500, a company must meet all of the following (per the published S&P 500 methodology):

  • U.S. company with primary listing on a U.S. exchange
  • Market capitalization above approximately $18 billion (this threshold is periodically reviewed)
  • At least 12 months of trading history since its initial public offering
  • Positive GAAP earnings in the most recent quarter and a positive sum of GAAP earnings over the four most recent quarters
  • Float of at least 50% of shares outstanding publicly tradeable
What this means in practice: A company that lists today and qualifies for the Nasdaq-100 fast-entry rule cannot simultaneously qualify for the S&P 500. The earliest it could be added to the S&P 500 is approximately 12 months after listing — and only if it has demonstrated GAAP profitability. Many high-growth technology companies are not GAAP-profitable, which may delay or preclude S&P 500 inclusion indefinitely.

This is not a ranking — it's a difference in what the indexes are designed to measure. The Nasdaq-100 is designed to capture the 100 largest Nasdaq-listed non-financials by market cap, with no profitability requirement. The S&P 500 is designed to represent the U.S. large-cap equity market with quality screens including profitability.

The result: the same company can appear in QQQ within weeks of its IPO but not in VOO for at least a year — if ever.

6. Total-market funds: a third timeline

Total-market funds like VTI (CRSP US Total Market) and ITOT (S&P Total Market) track broader indexes that include large, mid, small, and micro-cap stocks across all sectors. Their IPO inclusion policies differ from both the Nasdaq-100 and the S&P 500:

  • VTI / CRSP: New listings are generally added at the next quarterly reconstitution after the IPO. CRSP does not have a fast-entry rule equivalent to Nasdaq's.
  • ITOT / S&P Total Market: Governed by S&P DJI rules; follows a similar quarterly schedule. May add very large IPOs on a different schedule from the Nasdaq-100.

Total-market funds hold thousands of stocks, so a single new addition — even a large one — typically represents a smaller portfolio weight than it would in the 100-stock Nasdaq-100. The Exposure Analyzer lets you compare this across specific funds.

7. Target-date funds: the pass-through question

If your 401(k) is invested in a target-date fund (e.g., "2045 Fund" or "Retirement 2050"), you hold a fund-of-funds. The underlying funds — typically a mix of U.S. equity, international equity, and bond index funds — each have their own index methodology.

For Vanguard target-date funds, the domestic equity component is typically equivalent to VTI (CRSP US Total Market). It does not directly hold QQQ. Exposure to a Nasdaq-100 fast-entry addition passes through the total-market component at whatever weight that stock represents in the broader market — which is typically much smaller than its Nasdaq-100 weight.

For Fidelity Freedom funds, the composition includes both active and index underlying funds. The active components may or may not hold a given IPO, depending on portfolio manager decisions.

For T. Rowe Price Retirement funds, all underlying funds are actively managed. IPO exposure depends entirely on manager judgment.

The key question to ask: which underlying funds does my plan's target-date fund actually hold? Your plan's fund fact sheet or prospectus should list the underlying fund allocation.

8. Questions to ask

This site can describe the rules and timelines. What you do with that information is a personal decision, and one that may be informed by a conversation with a financial adviser or your plan's administrator. Here are questions that may be worth raising:

  • Which index does each fund on my plan menu track, and who is the index provider?
  • Does my plan menu include any Nasdaq-100 funds (QQQ, QQQM, or equivalents)?
  • For my target-date fund: what are the underlying funds, and which indexes do they track?
  • When would a company that lists this month first appear in each fund I hold, under current index rules?
  • How does a total-market fund's exposure to a new large-cap listing compare in magnitude to a Nasdaq-100 fund's?
  • What are the current lockup and float-expansion schedules for any large IPO I'm monitoring?
Where to verify information: Index methodology documents are published by each index provider (Nasdaq Index Services, S&P Dow Jones Indices, CRSP, FTSE Russell, MSCI). Fund holdings are published daily by ETF issuers (Invesco, Vanguard, iShares, State Street, Schwab, Fidelity). SEC EDGAR contains all exchange registration (8-A12B) and prospectus (424B4) filings.
